## 1. Measured accuracy

Not a claimed accuracy — a measured one. The engine derives tithi boundaries
from Meeus chapter 25 (Sun) and chapter 47 (Moon). Chapter 49 is an
independent series fitted directly to the moon phases, so comparing the two
at new and full moon measures real disagreement rather than repeating one
calculation twice.

Over **120 phases across 2024–2029**:

| quantity | value | in elongation | why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| mean signed error | 20.0 s | 10.1″ | a systematic bias, not noise |
| mean absolute error | 22.6 s | 11.5″ | typical case |
| worst case | 63.5 s | 32.2″ | the number to plan against |

Elongation grows at about 12.19°/day, so **one arcminute is two minutes of**
**time** at a tithi boundary. The worst case above is therefore around one
minute of boundary error.

### Where it degrades

- **The Sun is the weak half.** Meeus ch. 25 is stated at 0.01° (36″); the
  Moon series was checked against his Example 47.a and reproduces it to
  0.001″. The residual bias above is consistent with the solar term.
- **Aberration was worth 40 seconds.** Using geometric rather than apparent
  longitudes gave a 59 s mean error; applying the Sun's 20.5″ aberration
  brought it to 20 s. It is applied.
- **Sunrise is not better than a minute** whatever the ephemeris does,
  because standard refraction (34′) varies with temperature and pressure.
  For tithi-at-sunrise this, not the ephemeris, is the floor.
- **Two separate error budgets.** A tithi is a *difference* of longitudes,
  so nutation and ayanamsa cancel exactly and tithi dates carry ephemeris
  error only. Sankranti and month names are sidereal and additionally carry
  the Lahiri ayanamsa model, checked at the Calendar Reform Committee's
  defining point (23°15′00″ on 21 March 1956) where it reads 23°14′43″ —
  about 17″ low, or 7 minutes in the timing of an ingress.

## 2. Does an Adhik Maas occur in 2026?

**Yes.** The computation is unambiguous and the rule it uses is the standard
one: a lunar month containing no solar ingress is intercalary.

> **Adhika Jyeshtha, 2026-05-17 to 2026-06-15.** The Sun enters no sign during
> it. Vrishabha is entered during the preceding month and Mithuna during
> the following one, which is then Nija Jyeshtha.

### The 24 vs 26 question

Both numbers are right. They count different years, and the research
document appears to state one while tabulating the other.

| window | Ekadashis |
| --- | --- |
| Gregorian 2026 (1 Jan – 31 Dec) | **24** |
| Gregorian 2027 | 25 |
| Lunar year Chaitra 2026 – Phalguna (19 Mar 2026 – 7 Apr 2027) | **26**, over 13 months |

The reason is worth stating plainly, because it is the opposite of the
intuitive answer: **an Adhik Maas does not add Ekadashis to a Gregorian**
**year.** A Gregorian year contains about 12.37 lunations no matter what
they are called, so it always yields 24 or 25 Ekadashis. What the leap
month changes is their *names*. A compiler who misses Adhika Jyeshtha still
gets the right 24 dates but shifts every name from late May onwards by one
month — Padmini and Parama disappear, and what should be Nirjala lands on
the Padmini date.

The two Adhik Maas Ekadashis, computed:

| name | Smarta | Vaishnava | margin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Padmini | 2026-05-26 | 2026-05-27 | 13 min |
| Parama | 2026-06-11 | 2026-06-11 | 264 min |

The research document is quoted as giving Padmini on 27 May 2026 and Parama
on 11 June 2026. Parama agrees exactly. Padmini agrees with the **Vaishnava**
date; the Smarta date is a day earlier, because that tithi is current at two
consecutive sunrises. That is the Smarta/Vaishnava rule doing its job, not a
disagreement — but it does mean a single-date table has silently picked a
school.

mislabelled. Switching between window and instant changes no date in

the disagreement is about which day the tithi is assigned to, which is a
legitimate difference of rule and not an error.
